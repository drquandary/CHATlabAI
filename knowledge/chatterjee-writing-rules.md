# Anjan Chatterjee — Writing Rules

1. **Lead with the core finding, not the theory.** Start with the phenomenon, the problem,
   and the main payoff — not a grand theoretical frame. The paper is about how a perceptual
   feature / spatial form / aesthetic structure produces a measurable or interpretable effect.

2. **Make the interpretive payoff explicit.** The reader must know exactly what the analysis adds.
   Bad: "This framework opens new possibilities for integrating neuroaesthetics and anthropology."
   Better: "The analysis shows that monumental Maya masks did not only symbolize power. Their
   frontal faces, scale, and gaze-like organization likely worked as perceptual devices that made
   divine or political presence feel immediate."

3. **Do not overclaim mechanism.** Archaeology/behavior is *consistent with* perceptual/affective
   systems; it does not *prove* a neural mechanism.
   USE: may have recruited · likely intensified · is consistent with · helps explain · offers a
   plausible account of.
   AVOID: proves · demonstrates that [architects] knew · caused viewers to · directly activated ·
   hardwired response.

4. **Separate evidence from interpretation.** Keep the chain clean: (1) what is documented?
   (2) what is perceptually salient? (3) what does research make plausible? (4) what remains
   interpretive/speculative? The primary evidence carries the claim first; neuroscience sharpens
   the interpretation, it does not replace the evidence.

5. **Prefer convergence over single-study claims.** Several weak-to-moderate converging lines
   (monumentality, frontal symmetry, elevated placement, facial configuration, restricted viewing,
   ritual-political setting) beat one strong single-effect claim.

6. **Keep global patterns primary.** Emphasize global metrics and convergence patterns before
   node-level claims. Use global structure, bootstrap intervals, consensus/convergence-node
   analysis as the main result; treat specific node centrality / fine local effects as exploratory
   unless the sample is large and stable.

7. **Put uncertainty into the prose** — inside the claim, not as a trailing caveat.
   e.g., "At this corpus size, node-level centrality should be treated as exploratory. The more
   stable result is the repeated convergence of sensory, affective, and interpretive features
   around the same experiential episodes."

8. **Cut inflated language.** Prefer clean, direct prose over theoretical excess.
   Replace: "These masks instantiate an ontologically charged neuroaesthetic apparatus through
   which perceptual salience mediates cosmopolitical presence."
   With: "These masks joined perceptual force to cosmological meaning. Their scale, frontal faces,
   and placement helped make sacred authority visible and felt."

9. **Do not let the cultural material sound like decoration.** Don't reduce culture to an example
   of a universal brain response. "Universal perceptual tendencies do not explain the masks by
   themselves. They help explain why culturally specific beings, places, and powers could become
   forceful in experience."

10. **Make the contribution sayable in one sentence.**
    e.g., "The article argues that monumental Maya masks worked because they joined culturally
    specific ideas of divine presence with perceptual features — frontal faces, scale, gaze-like
    organization — that made that presence compelling in embodied experience."

11. **Use modest, testable language** that could survive hostile review: specific enough to test,
    modest enough to defend. Not "ENO explains Maya ritual experience" but "ENO offers a way to
    describe how built forms could organize attention, affect, and culturally learned expectations
    in ritual settings."

12. **Let the reader see the analytic steps.** Paragraph shape: first sentence = concrete claim;
    middle = evidence and reasoning; final = interpretive payoff.

In practice: make the claim strong, the mechanism cautious, the prose clean, and the contribution
impossible to miss.
